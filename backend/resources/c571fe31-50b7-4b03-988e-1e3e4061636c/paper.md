## Microplastics in Drinking Water: Health Risks and Filtration Technologies

### Abstract

The proliferation of plastic waste has led to the contamination of global drinking water supplies with microplastics (MPs), defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 millimeters. This review synthesizes the current state of knowledge regarding the occurrence of MPs in drinking water, the associated potential health risks, and the efficacy of filtration technologies for their removal. Literature confirms the ubiquitous presence of MPs in both tap and bottled water, originating from the degradation of larger plastic items and direct industrial release. While the ability of smaller MPs (<5 µm) to translocate into human tissues raises significant public health concern, direct toxicological risks from ingestion remain unquantified and are considered an emerging area of research. The World Health Organization currently advises prioritizing known microbial and chemical hazards. Conventional drinking water treatment plants (DWTPs) demonstrate high removal efficiencies (>90%), but do not eliminate MPs completely, leaving a role for point-of-use (POU) filters as a final barrier. Key research gaps include the lack of globally comparable occurrence data, insufficient causal and longitudinal evaluation of mitigation strategies, and a need for systematic benchmarking of filtration technologies. A targeted research agenda is proposed to address these gaps and inform future public health policy.

### 1. Introduction

Over the past century, the global production and consumption of plastics have grown exponentially, resulting in a persistent and pervasive environmental pollutant: microplastics (Du et al., 2024; Rao et al., 2025). These particles, generally defined as being smaller than 5 mm in size, are now found in virtually every ecosystem on Earth, from remote mountain peaks to the deep ocean (Iqbal et al., 2025; Rao et al., 2025). Their discovery in essential resources, including air, food, and drinking water, has elevated the issue from an environmental concern to a significant public health question (Chang & Yang, 2025; Rahman et al., 2025).

The presence of microplastics in drinking water, confirmed in studies of both municipal tap water and commercial bottled water, is of particular importance due to the potential for chronic, lifelong exposure (Chang & Yang, 2025). This paper provides a comprehensive review of the current academic literature on this topic. It will first synthesize findings on the occurrence and characteristics of microplastics in drinking water sources. Second, it will examine the state of evidence on potential human health risks, which are currently an area of active but incomplete investigation. Third, it will assess the effectiveness of existing and emerging filtration technologies at both municipal and household scales. Finally, this paper will identify critical research gaps and propose a future research agenda to build a more robust evidence base for risk assessment and management.

### 2. Literature Review

#### 2.1. Occurrence and Characteristics of Microplastics in Drinking Water

Microplastics are a highly heterogeneous class of contaminants, varying in size, shape (e.g., fibers, fragments, spheres), and polymer composition. While the upper size limit is commonly defined as 5 mm, research is increasingly focused on smaller fractions, including particles under 5 micrometers (µm) and nanoplastics (<1 µm), which may pose greater biological risks (Du et al., 2024; Rahman et al., 2025). These particles enter water systems through two main pathways. "Primary" microplastics are manufactured for direct use in products like cosmetics or industrial abrasives, while "secondary" microplastics form from the environmental weathering and fragmentation of larger plastic debris, such as bottles, bags, and fishing gear (Mulhern, 2024; Rao et al., 2025). The degradation of plastic waste in rivers, lakes, and oceans is a primary contributor to the contamination of raw water sources (Du et al., 2024).

The inevitable result of this environmental persistence is the documented contamination of drinking water supplies worldwide. A comprehensive synthesis of studies in China, for example, detailed the occurrence and characteristics of microplastics in both tap and bottled water, highlighting the scale of the issue in a rapidly industrializing region (Chang & Yang, 2025). The chemical inertness and resistance to natural degradation that make plastic a useful material also ensure its long-term persistence in the environment, leading to continuous contamination of water sources (Rahman et al., 2025).

#### 2.2. Human Health Risks: An Emerging Concern

The detection of microplastics in drinking water has logically prompted concern over their potential impact on human health (Rahman et al., 2025; Rao et al., 2025). The primary risk pathways under investigation include particle toxicity, chemical toxicity from leaching additives (e.g., plasticizers, flame retardants), and the role of microplastics as vectors for other environmental pollutants. Research suggests that particles, particularly those in the smaller micrometer range, have the potential to translocate across the gut barrier and enter biological systems (Du et al., 2024). This has led to specific concerns for vulnerable populations, with emerging research investigating the potential impacts of microplastic exposure on infant health (Du et al., 2024).

Despite these plausible risks, the direct toxicological impact on humans from ingestion via drinking water is not yet fully understood or quantified. One modeling study described the quantification of microplastic toxicity as an "open issue" (Guerrini et al., 2021). A significant portion of the risk may be indirect; microplastics in aquatic environments readily adsorb heavy metals and persistent organic pollutants, potentially acting as vectors that transport these harmful chemicals into the food chain and, ultimately, to humans (Guerrini et al., 2021).

Reflecting this state of uncertainty, the World Health Organization (WHO) advises that, based on current evidence, water suppliers and regulators should continue to prioritize the management of microbial pathogens and chemical contaminants known to pose significant health risks (World Health Organization, 2022). While acknowledging the presence of microplastics, the consensus is that they represent an emerging, rather than a proven, risk in the context of drinking water, pending further research.

#### 2.3. Filtration and Removal Technologies

In response to public concern, significant attention has been directed toward technologies for removing microplastics from water. At the municipal level, modern drinking water treatment plants (DWTPs) represent the first and most critical line of defense. Conventional treatment processes, including coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, and filtration, are effective at removing particulate matter in general and have been shown to be highly efficient at removing microplastics. Studies and reviews indicate that well-operated DWTPs can achieve removal efficiencies ranging from 70% to over 90% (Mulhern, 2024; World Health Organization, 2022). The WHO recommends that water suppliers focus on optimizing these existing particle removal processes as part of a comprehensive water safety plan (World Health Organization, 2022).

Despite these high removal rates, studies confirm that some microplastics, particularly smaller fragments and fibers, can persist through the treatment train and remain in finished tap water (Leusch et al., 2023). This residual contamination has spurred interest in point-of-use (POU) filtration devices as a final barrier at the household level. Research has begun to evaluate the efficacy of various POU technologies, such as activated carbon blocks and reverse osmosis systems, demonstrating their potential to further reduce consumer exposure to microplastics present in tap water (Leusch et al., 2023; LifeStraw, n.d.).

### 3. Research Gaps and Future Directions

While the literature establishes a strong foundation, it also reveals critical gaps that must be addressed to advance from problem identification to effective, evidence-based management. Four key areas require focused research.

First, there is a **lack of external validity in occurrence data due to geographic bias and methodological inconsistency** (Gap 1). Much of the existing data comes from specific regions, such as China (Chang & Yang, 2025), limiting the ability to generalize findings to cities with different infrastructure and consumption patterns. A future research priority should be the establishment of a global monitoring network using standardized protocols to generate comparable data across diverse urban and socioeconomic contexts.

Second, the evidence for the effectiveness of mitigation strategies suffers from an **insufficient use of causal evaluation methods** (Gap 2). Reports of high removal efficiencies at treatment plants are often based on simple before-and-after measurements at a single facility, which do not robustly establish causality or quantify uncertainty (World Health Organization, 2022). Future work should employ quasi-experimental designs to rigorously evaluate the impact of infrastructure upgrades and other interventions against a proper counterfactual baseline.

Third, the current evidence base is characterized by a **weakness in the longitudinal validation of proposed strategies** (Gap 3). Most studies are short-term "snapshots" and do not assess the performance and durability of filtration technologies over multiple seasons or during extreme weather events, which can significantly impact water quality. Longitudinal monitoring studies are needed to confirm that mitigation strategies are robust and reliable over time.

Finally, there is **limited systematic, comparative data on filtration technologies** (Gap 4). While it is known that filtration works, there is a scarcity of head-to-head studies that benchmark the performance of different technologies (e.g., reverse osmosis, carbon filtration, membrane filtration) against microplastics of varying sizes, shapes, and polymer types. This gap hinders the ability of municipalities and consumers to make informed decisions. A dedicated research program to systematically benchmark both DWTP and POU technologies is essential for developing targeted and effective removal strategies.

### 4. Conclusion

The contamination of drinking water with microplastics is a global reality, stemming from the unabated production and disposal of plastic goods (Rao et al., 2025). The scientific literature confirms their presence in water sources worldwide, raising plausible concerns about long-term human health effects, although direct toxicological risks remain an "open issue" requiring further investigation (Guerrini et al., 2021). Current expert guidance rightly prioritizes the management of known health risks in drinking water while research into microplastic toxicity continues (World Health Organization, 2022).

Fortunately, existing water treatment infrastructure provides a formidable, albeit incomplete, barrier. Centralized treatment plants are highly effective at removing the majority of microplastics, and optimizing these systems is a primary recommended strategy. The persistence of some particles post-treatment highlights a complementary role for point-of-use filtration devices in further reducing exposure. To move forward, the research community must focus on closing the critical knowledge gaps identified, particularly by generating globally comparable occurrence data, conducting rigorous causal and longitudinal evaluations of interventions, and systematically benchmarking filtration technologies. Addressing these gaps is paramount for developing sound public health policies and ensuring the safety of drinking water in a world increasingly saturated with plastic.
